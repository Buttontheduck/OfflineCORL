# EqM Actor for Deep RL

## Objective
Use **Equilibrium Matching (EqM)** as the **actor** (policy) of deep RL algorithms.

The action is generated by **time-unconditional iterative denoising**:
1. Start from Gaussian noise.
2. Refine the action by gradient descent on a learned, state-conditioned EqM field `f_θ(a | s)`.
3. Stop when it settles at an equilibrium, i.e. a point where the field is close to zero.

There is no time or noise-level input and no fixed integration schedule.

Target settings:
- **Offline RL** (current focus)
- **Offline-to-online RL** (later)

Background on every paper used here is in [`EqM_literature.md`](EqM_literature.md).

**Name:** the EqM-actor algorithm is called **Aware** for now (code: `Aware_Minari/`).

## Why EqM as an actor
- **Expressive:** it can represent multimodal action distributions, like diffusion and flow policies.
- **Time-free sampling:** step size and number of steps are free choices; it supports adaptive early stopping (stop when `‖f‖` is small) and warm starts.
- **One model, one energy landscape:** zeros of the field ≈ dataset actions for the given state (EqM, Statements 1–2).
- **Hypothesis (to test later):** the field norm `‖f(a|s)‖`, or the EqM energy, is a built-in **OOD signal**. It could keep the policy inside the data support, or filter candidate actions, without an extra density model or ensemble. GeCO and EqC report this kind of signal for robot control; it has not yet been tested in offline RL. See the low-dimensional caveat below.

## Caveat: low-dimensional actions (one action, no chunking)
EqM's guarantees (EqM paper, Appendix C) are **high-dimensional** arguments. Our actor generates **one action** $a \in \mathbb R^{d_a}$ per step, with no chunking, and $d_a$ is small (HalfCheetah: $d_a = 6$). This is exactly where those arguments are weakest.

Notation follows the paper, with $\rho$ in place of its $\gamma$. $x$ is a data sample (an action; condition everything on $s$ in RL), $d$ is its dimension, $\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I_d)$, and $x_\rho = \rho x + (1-\rho)\epsilon$.

### What Appendix C shows
**Step 0: MSE gives a conditional mean.** With perfect training, the learned field is the posterior mean of the target:

$$ f^*(z) = \mathbb E\big[\,c(\rho)\,(\epsilon - x)\ \big|\ x_\rho = z\,\big]. $$

**Statement 1 (C.1): the field is zero at the data.** Take a finite dataset $\mathcal X = \{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}\}$. For $\rho < 1$,

$$ p(x_\rho = z \mid x, \rho) = \mathcal N\big(z;\ \rho x,\ (1-\rho)^2 I_d\big), $$

while $\rho = 1$ puts a point mass on each $x^{(i)}$. At $z = x^{(i)}$ the paper argues that the $\rho < 1$ density is "exponentially small in $d$", so

$$ P\big(\rho = 1 \mid x_\rho = x^{(i)}\big) \to 1 \quad (d \to \infty) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad f^*(x^{(i)}) \approx c(1)\,(\epsilon - x^{(i)}) = 0 . $$

**Statement 2 (C.2): local minima are data.** If $f(\hat x) = 0$, then $\mathbb E[c(\rho)(\epsilon - x) \mid x_\rho = \hat x] = 0$. Since $c(\rho) > 0$ for $\rho < 1$, the paper argues that in high $d$ this vector average can only vanish if the posterior concentrates on $\rho = 1$. That gives $P(\hat x \in \mathcal X) \approx 1$.

**Statement 3 (C.3): convergence of gradient descent.** If $f = \nabla E$, $E$ is $L$-smooth and bounded below, and $\eta \le 1/L$, then $\min_{k<K} \lVert f(x_k) \rVert^2 \le 2\,(E(x_0) - E_{\inf}) / (\eta K)$.

### Why this breaks for a low-dimensional action
**1. No concentration in low $d$.** The posterior weight of a candidate source $(x^{(j)}, \rho)$ at a point $z$ is

$$ w_j(\rho \mid z) \ \propto\ (1-\rho)^{-d} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert z - \rho\, x^{(j)} \rVert^2}{2(1-\rho)^2}\Big). $$

The squared distance sums $d$ coordinates, so in high $d$ every source other than the matching one is suppressed like $e^{-O(d)}$. At $d_a = 6$ that suppression is mild. Many $(x^{(j)}, \rho)$ pairs carry comparable weight, so $f^*(z)$ is a real average of many different target vectors, not the single $\rho = 1$ term.

At an *exact* training point the self term can still dominate for any $d$, but only if the network memorizes. An RL actor should generalize, so the relevant object is the population optimum for a continuous $p(a \mid s)$.

**2. The population optimum is a mean-shift field, which is not zero on the data.** Since $x_\rho - x = (1-\rho)(\epsilon - x)$, the target can be rewritten as

$$ c(\rho)\,(\epsilon - x) = \frac{c(\rho)}{1-\rho}\,(x_\rho - x). $$

For truncated decay with multiplier $\lambda$ and $\rho > a_0$, the factor is constant: $c(\rho)/(1-\rho) = \lambda/(1-a_0) =: k$. With the current config ($\lambda = 8$, $a_0 = 0.8$), $k = 40$. Near the data, where the $\rho > a_0$ sources dominate the posterior,

$$ f^*(z) \ \approx\ k\,\big(z - \mathbb E[x \mid z]\big) \ \approx\ -k\,\sigma^2\, \nabla_z \log p_\sigma(z), $$

using Tweedie's formula, where $\sigma$ is the effective local noise scale and $p_\sigma$ is the noise-smoothed data density. This field is zero only where $\nabla \log p_\sigma = 0$, i.e. at modes, not on the whole data support.

**3. Tangential drift.** Near the data manifold $\mathcal M$, split $f^*$ into two parts:
- a **normal** part $f_n$, which pulls points onto $\mathcal M$ (wanted);
- a **tangential** part $f_t \approx -k\sigma^2\, \nabla_T \log p$, which slides points *along* $\mathcal M$ toward higher density.

Because $f_t \neq 0$ on $\mathcal M$, every gradient-descent step also moves the action along the support. With many steps, or a large $\eta$, samples collapse toward the densest modes of the (advantage-weighted) action distribution instead of sampling it.

**4. Cancellation creates false equilibria.** Write $f^*(z) = \mathbb E\big[\tfrac{c(\rho)}{1-\rho}(z - x) \mid z\big]$. Each candidate source $x$ "votes" with a vector pointing from $x$ to $z$. When likely sources lie on opposite sides of $z$, for example between two action modes, the votes cancel and $f^* \approx 0$ *off* the data.

This is exactly where Statement 2's step "stationary ⇒ data" fails. By symmetry, the midpoint of two modes has $f^* = 0$. In theory these points are saddles, but a learned field can turn them into weak basins or "bridges" where samples stop.

**5. The field is not a true gradient.** Statement 3 needs $f = \nabla E$. The implicit $f$ is an unconstrained network output, and its Jacobian $J_f$ is clearly not symmetric in the 2D toy. So the convergence bound, and reading $\lVert f \rVert$ as an energy gradient, are assumptions here, not guarantees.

### Consequences for this project
- **We are in the low-$d$ regime.** One $6$-D action per step is low-$d$. The robotics EqM papers (GeCO, π0-EqM, EqC) all generate **action chunks** ($D$ = 112 to 700), which is much closer to the paper's assumptions.
- **State conditioning only partly helps.** It helps when $p(a \mid s)$ is concentrated (EqC's argument). Mixed-quality datasets (medium-replay, expert + random) are multimodal per state, which is where drift and cancellation bite.
- **For the OOD hypothesis:** a small $\lVert f(a\mid s) \rVert$ does **not** imply in-distribution in low $d$ (cancellation zeros, bridges). And in-distribution actions need not have $\lVert f \rVert \approx 0$ (tangential drift). The OOD score has to be validated empirically, not assumed from Statement 1.
- **Practical knobs:** bound the total step budget $\eta K$, or use early stopping. Check the final actions against the dataset's action modes. Candidate fixes are Jacobian-gated samplers, a Fourier lift of the action, and action chunking (see below).

### 2D toy experiments (short)
Full details in [`EqM_2d_toy.md`](EqM_2d_toy.md).

- **Setup:** 2D moons; implicit EqM with an MLP 128×3, $\lambda = 4$, $a_0 = 0.8$; Nesterov sampler with $\eta = 10^{-3}$; 5000 samples. Metric is SWD to the test set (lower is better). One seed.
- **Drift:** the plain sampler collapses as steps increase. SWD is 0.110 at 250 steps, 0.624 at 1000, and 1.259 at 1000 steps with $3\eta$.
- **Jacobian-gated samplers:** these split normal from tangent directions using the eigenvalues of $\mathrm{sym}(J_f)$, with no retraining.
  - Normal projection: 0.210.
  - Tangential diffusion: **0.147** (best).
  - Tangential Langevin: no help.
  - The inner-left part of the inner moon stays uncovered. That is a property of the learned field, which no sampler can fix.
- **Cancellation fix (lifting):** lift the data with fixed Fourier features $\phi_j(x) = \sin(W_j^\top x + b_j)$, train in $2+m$ dimensions, and keep the first 2 coordinates.
  - Zero-valued extra dimensions (no information about $x$) never help.
  - $+32$ Fourier dimensions at $w = 6$ (1000 epochs): SWD **0.519 → 0.038**, both moons fully covered with the plain sampler.
  - The gain tracks $s = \sqrt{1 + m w^2 / 2}$: $s \approx 4$ fails, $8$ to $24$ works, $48$ gets worse.
- **Relevance to RL:** the Fourier lift needs only the sample itself, so it applies directly to a single low-dimensional action.
- **Not done yet:** multiple seeds, and direct measurement of cancellation.

## Phase 1 (current): make offline RL with an EqM actor work
Before any OOD study, the baseline has to work: an EqM actor that learns a good offline policy.

### Base algorithm: AWAC
Why AWAC:
- **Swappable actor loss:** the actor update is weighted regression on dataset actions. It only needs a per-sample loss, so `log π(a|s)` can be replaced by the EqM loss. No likelihood and no backprop through the sampling chain are needed.
- **Stays in support:** AWAC's implicit KL constraint to the behavior policy keeps the EqM actor near the data, without fitting a separate behavior model.
- **Fine-tuning ready:** AWAC was designed for offline pretraining followed by online fine-tuning, which gives a direct path to Phase 3.

### EqM-AWAC (working name: Aware)
`ρ` is the EqM interpolation factor, written γ in the EqM paper; renamed here to avoid a clash with the discount.

**Actor:** the EqM field `f_θ(x | s)`
```
x_ρ    = ρ·a + (1 − ρ)·ε,      ρ ~ U(0,1),  ε ~ N(0, I),  (s, a) ~ D
target = λ · c(ρ) · (ε − a),    c(ρ) = min(1, (1 − ρ)/(1 − a₀))      # truncated decay
w(s,a) = min(exp(A(s,a)/T), w_max)
L_actor = E[ w(s,a) · ‖f_θ(x_ρ | s) − target‖² ]
```

**Advantage:**
```
A(s,a) = min(Q₁,Q₂)(s,a) − V(s)
V(s)   = mean over a_π ~ π_EqM(·|s) of min(Q₁,Q₂)(s, a_π)
```

**Critic:** clipped double-Q TD with target networks.
```
y = r + γ·(1 − d)·min(Q̄₁,Q̄₂)(s', a'),   a' ~ π_EqM(·|s')
```

**Sampling `π_EqM`:**
```
a₀ ~ N(0, I);   repeat K times:  a ← a − η·f_θ(a | s)   (plain GD or Nesterov)
clip a to the action bounds
```

**Why the weighted EqM loss is a valid policy update:**
- For each `(x, s)`, the minimizer is the weighted conditional mean of the target.
- So the learned field is the EqM field of the reweighted policy `π ∝ π_β · exp(A/T)`. This is the same argument as QIPO's energy-weighted flow matching.
- AWAC drops the per-state normalizer `Z(s)`. This changes only how much each *state* counts in the loss, not which actions are preferred within a state.

### Current implementation
- **Code:** `Aware_Minari/` (Minari data) and `Aware/` (D4RL data), same algorithm; see *Code structure* below.
- **Data:** Minari datasets, currently `mujoco/halfcheetah/simple-v0`; D4RL `halfcheetah-medium-v2` for server runs.
- **Gaussian AWAC reference:** `Awac_Minari.py` and `Offline Awac/`.
- **Defaults at time of writing:**
  - Field schedule: truncated c with `a₀ = 0.8`, `λ = 8`.
  - Weights: `T = 1.0`, `w_max = 100`.
  - Sampler: Nesterov, `η = 0.01`, `K = 20`, momentum 0.3.
  - Training: 4×256 MLPs, batch 256, lr 3e-4, target τ = 5e-3, 120k steps (`num_train_ops: 120001`), `device: cuda`.
- **Options already in the code:**
  - FINO noise injection.
  - Early-stopping samplers.
  - OOD-aware sampling: pick the candidate with the lowest final `‖f‖`, with moving-average or leaky-bucket scoring.
  - Reject-and-rank at evaluation: drop candidates above an OOD threshold, then pick by Q.

### Things to verify in Phase 1
- EqM-AWAC matches or beats Gaussian AWAC on the same Minari datasets (normalized return; see the dataset note above for what this score means).

**Interpretation**
1. **Aware trains.** The EqM actor reaches the same final return as Gaussian AWAC, and its critic is stable. Aware's Q (~745) matches its actual return rate, 7.47 per step / (1 − γ) ≈ 747, so there is no sign of Q overestimation on out-of-distribution actions. Phase 1's first goal, a working offline EqM actor, is met on this dataset.
2. **The gain over the data is probably "denoised behavior cloning", not RL.** Both methods end about 8% above the dataset average and above its best episode. AWAC evaluates with the Gaussian mean, and EqM's descent to an equilibrium lands near a mode of the action distribution. Either way, the policy reproduces the behavior policy without its noise. On a narrow, single-policy dataset (std 413), that alone can explain the gain.
3. **Aware's advantage weighting is much milder than AWAC's.** Behavior cloning is the case where every data action gets the same weight.
   - Aware's weights are moderately spread (median ≈ 0.7, std ≈ 0.5–1), so about half of each batch effectively counts.
   - AWAC's weights are widely spread (std ≈ 7–17), so a few high-advantage samples dominate, and only about 5–15% of each batch effectively counts.
   - Aware therefore sits between BC and AWAC: it still prefers the better data actions, but less strongly.
   - The advantage is negative on average because V, computed from EqM samples, is higher than Q at dataset actions; the critic rates EqM actions above the data.
   - A lower `T` would make the weighting sharper.
4. **Why the two Q values differ.** AWAC's TD targets use actions *sampled* from its Gaussian, which are worse than the mean it evaluates with, so its critic values a noisier policy (~655) than the one it evaluates. Aware uses the same EqM sampler for its TD targets and for evaluation, so its critic and its evaluation measure the same policy.
5. **Early training is slower and noisier for Aware.** Part of this is eval noise (1 episode vs 10), but some early single-episode returns (463, 1550, 2682) are real failed episodes. The EqM field needs more updates before its samples are reliable.
6. **Cost.** Aware is about 3.4× slower in wall-clock, from 20 sampler steps for every V(s) and TD-target action, plus slow batch-1 evaluation on MPS.

**What this does *not* show:** that EqM is better than a Gaussian actor. `simple-v0` is unimodal and narrow, so it can't reward expressiveness.

**Next steps**
- **BC ablation:** Aware with weights fixed at 1 (the commented line in `agents.py`). If it also reaches ~7,470, the advantage weighting isn't contributing here.
- **Harder data:** Minari `medium-v0` (std 3,009), and a mixed simple + medium + expert dataset. Multimodal data is where an EqM actor should beat a single Gaussian.
- **Fair protocol:** the same number of eval episodes (10) for both, the same network depth, 3–5 seeds, and the same number of steps (120k for now).
- **Tuning:** a lower `T` to make the advantage weighting sharper.

### Experiment 2: λ sweep on Minari simple-v0 and medium-v0 (3 seeds, 2026-11-03)

**Interpretation**
1. **The λ ranking is a sampler-budget effect, not a property of the learned field.**
   - The field's slope scales with λ (≈ λ far from the data, ≈ 5λ near it), so the sampler's progress is set by `η·λ·K`.
   - At `η·λ·K = 0.8` both λ = 2 (K = 40) and λ = 4 (K = 20) give ≈ 3,200–3,400. From ≈ 1.6 upward (λ = 8 with K = 20, λ = 4 with K = 40, λ = 2 with K = 100) every λ reaches ≈ 7,200–7,450 on simple.
   - With K = 20, λ = 2 and 4 never converge. Training suffers too: V(s) and the TD targets come from those bad samples, so Q overestimates the policy and 17–39% of the weights sit at the cap.
2. **Collapse, the low-dimensional symptom, is confirmed.** Once converged, each state's samples are ≈ 10× narrower than the dataset's spread, and every noise starting point ends at nearly the same action.
   - The field is clearly nonzero at dataset actions (‖f‖ ≈ 3–6) and ≈ 0 only at the sampler endpoints. The data are not equilibria; the field has one zero per state, at the mode.
   - This is the mean-shift picture from the low-dimensional caveat (Statement 1 fails).
   - Aware therefore acts as a **deterministic, mode-seeking policy** ("denoised BC"), not a sampler. It loses the expressiveness that motivates EqM.
   - For Phase 2 it means ‖f‖ measures distance from the mode, not from the data support: dataset actions score high, and the actor's own outputs score ≈ 0.
3. **No harmful drift on these datasets.** The sampler stops: movement → 0 by K ≈ 1000. On simple the return is flat from K = 20 to 1000. Both datasets come from one TQC policy each, so there is a single mode to drift to, and cancellation between modes cannot be tested here.
4. **Why simple works and medium looks unstable.**
   - **Simple:** a slow (7.4 m/s), consistent gait with almost no flips. A mode-seeking policy is close to ideal there and robust to small action errors.
   - **Medium:** a fast gait (13.7 m/s) at the edge of stability. The behavior policy itself flips in 20.6% of episodes, so returns are bimodal.
   - **Converged medium policy (λ = 4, K = 100):** learns the gait (12,559 without a flip, vs the data's 13,373) but also inherits the flips (30% of episodes). The advantage weighting does not remove them; the critic, trained on the K = 20 policy, overestimates.
   - **As trained (K = 20):** the unconverged actions cannot sustain the fast gait (2.9 m/s).
   - **1-episode evals:** each eval shows one mode or the other, so the medium curves jump by thousands; simple's tight returns hide this.
5. **Medium with λ = 8 is a separate failure.** It isn't under-convergence (‖f‖ ≈ 0.1 by K = 100) or drift (bad at every K, all 3 seeds): the policy only reaches ≈ 2.4 m/s. Its endpoints leave the action box more often (21% of dims vs 8–11%), but the cause is unknown.

**Answers to "Things to verify in Phase 1"** (this data only)
- **The sampler converges:** not with K = 20 for λ ≤ 4. Use `η·λ·K ≳ 1.6`.
- **Collapse:** yes.
- **Drift:** no.
- **Clip bounds:** 8–21% of action dimensions at convergence.

**Next steps**
- **Retrain with a converged sampler** (λ = 4 with K = 40–100, or λ = 8 with K = 20), so the critic and the weights see converged actions; then compare λ fairly.
- **10 eval episodes on medium**: with bimodal returns, 1-episode evals mostly measure flip luck.
- **Gaussian AWAC on medium**: if it flips ≈ 30% too, the flips come from imitating this dataset, not from the EqM actor.
- **Mixed simple + medium + expert data**: the only setting here where collapse would cost return, and where EqM should beat a Gaussian.
- **Log the per-state sample spread and the final ‖f‖ during training**, so collapse and non-convergence show up in W&B.

**Interpretation**
1. **Medium's failure comes from the weighting, not the EqM actor (#1).**
   - With BC weights, Aware reaches 11,916, about the dataset average (12,089); each seed's best eval is ≈ 14,500. The EqM actor can learn the fast gait.
   - With advantage weights, every failing medium run has the same signature: the critic overestimates the policy's value 3.5–14×, and the weights use only ≈ 3% of each batch (or 39% sit at the cap at K = 20).
   - The BC run's critic is calibrated (Q / implied ≈ 1.05), because its policy ≈ the behavior policy.
   - Its 10-episode evals still spread widely (std 3,222, min 4,883), so it probably inherits the dataset's flips; this is not measured.
2. **On simple, the weighting helps a little.** BC weights give 7,097 (+2.5% over the data); the weighted runs give 7,389–7,491 (+7–8%). The critic is calibrated there.
3. **The `η·λ·K` rule holds in training (#2).**
   - λ = 2 with η = 0.04 jumps from 296 (Exp 2) to 7,491. It matches λ = 8 at equal steps, with a calibrated critic and mild weights.
   - So λ only matters through `η·λ·K`.
   - On medium, K = 40 and η = 0.02 give the same result (2,742 vs 3,149), so K and η are interchangeable too.
4. **A converged sampler does not fix medium (#2).**
   - At equal steps it is no better than K = 20.
   - In Exp 2 (diagnostic A), the K = 20-trained λ = 4 checkpoints reached 9,671–10,160 when *evaluated* with K = 40–100. *Training* with converged samples gives only ≈ 3,000.
   - The converged samples feed V(s) and the TD targets. The critic then overestimates more (≈ 5× vs 3.5×), the advantage of dataset actions turns negative (mean ≈ −2.6), and the weights collapse onto ≈ 3% of the batch.
5. **Gaussian AWAC (#3):**
   - **simple:** it ties Aware (7,537 vs 7,491).
   - **medium:** it reaches only 7,207 (60% of the data). Its critic overestimates too (1.7×), but its weights keep a larger effective batch (16%).
   - Its evals swing widely: std over the last 20 evals is 1,797, and each seed's best eval is 9,805–11,814.
   - So the weighting problem on medium is not specific to EqM. The EqM actor is hit much harder by it, yet the EqM actor with BC weights beats Gaussian AWAC by ≈ 4,700.
6. **D4RL (#4): Gaussian AWAC wins clearly on medium-expert; Aware wins slightly on medium-replay.**
   - **The baseline is sound.** After 151k steps, AWAC (93.4 and 45.2) already matches CORL's published 1M-step scores (95.0 and 45.1).
   - **medium-expert: AWAC 93.4 vs Aware 59.5.**
     - AWAC reaches the best dataset episode (92.9), i.e. it imitates the expert half of the data.
     - Aware ends between CORL's BC (56.0) and the dataset average (64.4).
     - Aware also learns far more slowly (734 vs 7,783 at 20k), varies more across seeds (std 8.0 vs 0.2), its evals swing ≈ 5× more, and it is still rising at 151k.
   - **medium-replay: Aware 46.9 vs AWAC 45.2.** The gap is small but consistent across seeds (std 0.3 each). Both are above the best dataset episode (42.4), and Aware matches CORL AWAC's 1M-step score at 15% of the steps.
   - **The gap follows Aware's critic, as on Minari medium.**
     - Where it overestimates, Aware loses. On medium-expert: 1.7×, ≈ 3.6% effective batch, and the sharpest weights of all runs (median 0.14).
     - Where it is calibrated, Aware matches or beats AWAC. On medium-replay: 0.8×.
   - **Collapse alone does not explain medium-expert.**
     - AWAC is evaluated with its Gaussian mean, so it also outputs one action per state, and it still scores 93.4.
     - What differs is where the weighted fit lands: AWAC's lands on the expert behavior, Aware's ends between the medium and expert returns.
7. **Medium-expert controls: the gap is in the weighting, not in imitation.**
   - **BC weights: 50.1.** That is close to CORL's Gaussian BC (56.0 ± 7.4, though after 1M steps), between the medium (40.7) and expert (88.1) halves of the data, nearer the medium half. Imitation accounts for at most a few points of the gap to AWAC.
   - **What the weighting adds:** +9 points for Aware (50.1 → 59.5), vs ≈ +37 for the Gaussian (CORL BC 56.0 → AWAC 93.4).
   - **T = 1/3: 53.4,** no better than T = 1 (59.5; the difference is not significant with 3 seeds).
     - With T = 1/3, most weights are ≈ 0, so Aware copies the few samples per batch its critic ranks highest.
     - Gaussian AWAC uses the same formula and temperature and reaches 93.4. Sharp weighting works when the ranking is right, so Aware's ranking picks out the wrong actions.
8. **Open: is the critic the root cause, or the sampler (tangential drift / off-support endpoints)?**
   - **The critic's ranking is the proximate cause.** Every failing run overestimates, and BC weights, which ignore the ranking, avoid the failure.
   - **But the sampler endpoints track the failures too.** In every failing or weak run, the endpoints leave the action box ≈ 2× as often as the data sits at its bounds. In every good run the rate is ≤ 1.1× (table above).
     - V(s) and the TD targets query the critic at exactly these endpoints, so off-support samples could be what makes it overestimate.
     - The overestimated values then sharpen the weights onto the wrong actions, which reshapes the field. That is a possible feedback loop.
   - **What existing data says about "too many / too big steps":**
     - On Minari (Exp 2, diagnostic A/B), more steps never hurt (flat from K = 20 to 1000; movement → 0).
     - But Minari has one behavior policy per dataset, and medium-expert is the first per-state multimodal data, where the low-dimensional caveat predicts drift and cancellation to bite. So drift is untested exactly where it matters.
   - **How to separate the two:** evaluate the medium-expert checkpoints with different step budgets (next steps).
     - If off-box endpoints and Q at the sampler's outputs grow with `η·K`, and fewer or smaller steps help, the sampler is the driver.
     - If the endpoints are already off-support at moderate K, it is the learned field.
     - If neither holds, it is the critic itself.
   - **Answered by the drift diagnostics** (next section): neither. Extra or smaller steps change nothing once the sampler has converged, and both critics agree on the actions at dataset states. The weighted policy fails in closed loop: it flips.

**Answers to the Experiment 3 questions**
- **#1, does the weighting help?** A little on simple (+300–400). On medium it is what breaks Aware: BC 11,916 vs weighted λ = 8 1,280.
- **#2, does the `η·λ·K` rule hold in training?** Yes.
- **#2, does a converged critic help medium?** No.
- **#3, does a Gaussian policy inherit the flips?** Still open. `Awac_Minari.py` logs only the mean of the 10 episodes, so the checkpoints in `/workspace/Trained_Models/checkpoints/AWAC_env_namemedium-v0_*` need a re-evaluation with the diagnostic C rollout code.

  - The controls put the gap in the weighting, not in imitation: BC weights 50.1, T = 1/3 53.4.
  - The drift diagnostics (next section) show that the weighting does reach the expert gait, but the weighted policy flips in ≈ 60% of episodes.

**Interpretation**
1. **Too many steps are ruled out.**
   - On dataset states the sampler converges and stops: ‖f‖ → 0 and movement → 0 by K ≈ 80–160. After that the endpoints don't change (outside the box, nn ratio and ΔQ are flat up to K = 2,560).
   - In rollouts, K = 40–1,280 gives the same returns and flip rates as K = 20, within noise.
   - So there is no tangential drift that continues with more steps.
2. **Too large steps hurt only at η = 0.08,** where the sampler oscillates. That costs every run, including BC and medium-replay. The trained η = 0.01 is in the stable range: η = 0.0025–0.04 behave alike once `η·K` ≥ 0.2. Sampler tuning helps a little (best: 8,294) but leaves the gap to AWAC (11,203).
3. **The critic's ranking on dataset states is not the problem.**
   - Converged samples are on the data's support (nn ratio 0.96–1.0).
   - Both Aware's critic and the independent AWAC critic score them ≈ +4 above the data action. That is more than the AWAC critic gives the AWAC policy's own action (+3.2).
4. **The failure is in closed loop: flips.**
   - The weighted policy reaches the expert gait: episodes without a flip score 9,718 at 10.2 m/s, in the expert region 92% of the time. But it flips in 57–63% of episodes.
   - With BC weights it almost never flips (2%), but it stays in the medium region (19%) at 6.3 m/s.
   - Gaussian AWAC runs the expert gait (11.6 m/s) without flips (2%).
   - Sharper weights mean more flips: BC 2% → T = 1 57% → T = 1/3 63%.
   - This matches Minari medium-v0 (Exp 2, diagnostic C), where the weighted policy also flipped on the fast gait.
5. **What sets the flipping policies apart: saturated actions.**
   - The weighted fields converge to actions with 21% (T = 1) or 17% (T = 1/3) of dimensions beyond the box. That compares with 4.6% for BC weights and 2.6% for AWAC on the same states, and 3.0% for the expert data. On visited states the share is 15–19%, 5–6× the expert data's.
   - Each weighted endpoint is as far from the neighbouring actions as a single data sample (nn ratio ≈ 1), while BC-Aware (0.77) and AWAC (0.84) produce smoother, averaged actions.
   - This fits **mode-seeking**: EqM's sampler converges to the *mode* of the advantage-weighted action distribution, which sits at extreme, high-advantage actions. Gaussian AWAC regresses to its *weighted mean*.
   - It is the collapse half of the low-dimensional caveat (point 3: samples collapse toward the densest modes of the advantage-weighted distribution). But it happens *within* the trained step budget, as soon as the sampler converges, not through extra steps.
   - **Not shown:** that saturation *causes* the flips. Across the 198 settings, flip rate and saturation correlate (Spearman 0.54–0.65 for the weighted runs). But on medium-replay, Aware saturates as much (16%, data 12.5%) on a slow gait (5.9 m/s) and does not flip. So saturation seems to matter on the fast, near-unstable expert gait.
6. **The critic doesn't see the flips coming.**
   - On visited states it overestimates the weighted policies (1.34–1.46; 1.46–1.56 on episodes with a flip), while it is calibrated for BC (0.82), medium-replay (0.96) and AWAC's own critic (0.90).
   - The data contains almost no flipped states (0.25–0.6%), so no critic can learn to avoid flips from it. AWAC's policy just doesn't flip.
7. **Under-converged sampling avoids flips but is slow.** At `η·K` = 0.1, flips drop to 0–10%, but the return drops to ≈ 3.2k. The sampler's unconverged noise damps the gait along with the flips.

**Answer to interpretation 8.**
- Neither sampler drift from too many or too large steps, nor a wrong critic ranking at dataset states.
- The problem is on the actor side, in where the weighted field's sampler converges: saturated, mode-seeking actions that make the fast expert gait flip.
- The critic contributes by not foreseeing flips, as any critic trained on this data would fail to.

**Next steps**
- **Saturation test (evaluation only, cheap):** roll out the same checkpoints with the converged actions shrunk or clipped inward (e.g. 0.9·a, or clipped at ±0.9). Fewer flips and a higher return would show that saturation causes the flips.
- **Training-side, if saturation is causal:**
  - softer weights (T = 2–5) or a lower weight cap;
  - averaging the weighted targets so the fixed point moves toward the weighted mean;
  - a bounded action parameterization (e.g. tanh-squashed), so fixed points cannot leave the box. That one is a low-dimensionality-style change, so it needs the owner's approval.
- **Count flips on Minari medium-v0** for Gaussian AWAC and Aware with BC weights (Experiment 3 #3, still open). `drift.py` and `common.py` support Minari runs, but `drift.py` has only been run on D4RL so far.

#### 2. Shared IQL critic: test value learning separately from actor extraction

**Expectation:** an IQL-like critic could help substantially, but it is not guaranteed to close the gap. It removes one plausible failure mechanism: policy-generated actions entering value-learning targets. It does not guarantee that the extracted EqM policy will be stable during rollouts.

The proposed replacement is an expectile value fit and a value-based TD target:

$$
V(s) \leftarrow \operatorname{expectile}_{\tau_{\mathrm{exp}}}\!\left[Q(s,a),\ a\sim D(\cdot\mid s)\right],
\qquad y = r + \gamma(1-d)V(s').
$$

Extract the actor with fixed dataset weights:

$$
w(s,a) = \min\!\left\{\exp\!\left[\frac{Q(s,a)-V(s)}{T}\right],\ 100\right\}.
$$

This follows IQL's separation of value learning from policy extraction; see the [IQL paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06169).

**Controlled design:**
1. Train one IQL Q/V pair per seed on the same dataset and preprocessing as the comparison methods.
2. Freeze Q/V and compute fixed weights for dataset transitions.
3. Train a Gaussian actor and an EqM actor using those **identical weights**.
4. Match data, preprocessing, actor training budgets, and approximately network capacity. Report critic-pretraining and actor-training budgets separately. Check learning curves before interpreting a failure as structural rather than undertraining.
5. Start with expectile `τ_exp = 0.7`, as in the [authors' MuJoCo configuration](https://github.com/ikostrikov/implicit_q_learning/blob/master/configs/mujoco_config.py). Keep `T = 1` for the first comparison with current Aware; test `T = 1/3` separately so temperature is not silently changed alongside the critic. Here `T` divides the advantage, whereas the official configuration's `temperature = 3` is an inverse-temperature multiplier.
6. Log weight distributions, clipping fractions, and effective batch share. These help distinguish changes in action preferences from changes in weight sharpness.
7. **Second set of weights, from Aware's own critic.** For each seed, take the trained Aware medium-expert checkpoint (T = 1, #4) and freeze both its critics **and its EqM actor**. Compute the weights once with the training formula: V(s) from that actor's EqM sampler (η = 0.01, K = 20, momentum 0.3), averaged over many samples per state (e.g. 32; training used 1) with a fixed random seed, so V(s) is not noisy. Save them as **one cached weight array per seed**, and train both actors on that same array. Otherwise the random samples in V(s) would give the two actors slightly different targets.

| Frozen weights from | Gaussian actor | EqM actor |
|---|---|---|
| IQL critic | planned | planned |
| Aware's critic | added | added |

**Limits of the Aware-weights arm:**
- It tests the **final** weights only. During training the weights changed together with the critic and the actor; a refit on frozen final weights can't reproduce that feedback loop.
- Aware's weights are very concentrated (≈ 3.6% effective batch share). A failure on them can come from that concentration, from optimization, or from too little training, not only from a wrong ranking of actions.
- **Optional, matched concentration:** also refit on Aware's weights recomputed with a temperature chosen so that their effective batch share matches the IQL weights'. This separates *which* actions get weight from *how concentrated* the weight is.

| Outcome | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Both actors work well | The original critic–policy coupling or resulting weights likely contributed; this alone does not isolate which component. |
| Gaussian works; EqM fails | EqM fitting/sampling remains a problem under a shared target. |
| Both fail | Investigate the common target, training budget, and implementation before attributing failure to EqM. |
| EqM improves but still trails Gaussian | Both value learning and actor extraction may contribute. |
| Gaussian works with Aware's weights | Those weights support a successful Gaussian policy. This strengthens an actor-fitting/sampling explanation for EqM's failure. |
| Gaussian fails with Aware's weights but works with IQL's | The weight source matters. |
| Gaussian fails with Aware's weights | Does not alone show that Aware's critic ranks actions wrongly: weight concentration, optimization, and too little training can also cause it. |

**Complementary control: expert-only BC.** Train Gaussian BC and EqM BC on exactly the same expert-only trajectories, with all weights equal to one and no critic-dependent actor update. If Gaussian BC runs the expert gait reliably while EqM BC flips, the failure can occur without advantage weighting. This is a matched control; comparing EqM BC at 151k updates against published Gaussian BC at 1M updates cannot isolate imitation quality.

#### 3. Tangential drift: measure the movement and intervene on it

**Hypothesis:** after approaching the conditional action distribution, refinement moves actions along it in a way that worsens the resulting policy. A return-versus-K curve alone cannot establish this mechanism.

**Datasets.** Run the measurements and interventions below on:
- **`medium-expert` first: the main dataset,** because that is where the unexplained failure occurs.
  - Tangential drift does not need two separate behaviors. Within one behavior, samples can slide along a spread-out, anisotropic action distribution toward its peak.
  - That 99% of an expert state's 32 nearest neighbours are expert states shows that medium and expert states are well separated. It does not show that each state has a single action or no tangent directions.
  - Aware's per-state samples on medium-expert are the narrowest of all runs (spread ≈ 0.068), which is consistent with samples sliding toward the peak. Sliding toward the peak of the weighted distribution during the 20 sampler steps could also be what produces the saturated actions.
- **A controlled conditional-mixture dataset as a mechanism test** (new; needs a small script). It targets the case with two behaviors at the same state, where the sampler can pick one behavior and cancellation between them can create false stopping points (caveat points 3 and 4).
  - **Build it without switching policies:** collect a fixed bank of states from rollouts of two policies we already have, the medium-expert Gaussian AWAC (fast gait) and Aware with BC weights (medium gait). Then query both policies many times at every banked state and keep the (state, action) pairs. The conditional target is the mixture $p(a\mid s) = \tfrac12\,\pi_{\mathrm{AWAC}}(a\mid s) + \tfrac12\,\pi_{\mathrm{BC}}(a\mid s)$. The queried actions are never executed, so switching between gaits can't destabilize anything; this isolates distribution fitting.
  - **Fit it with BC:** train the EqM actor with all weights equal to one, using the actor-only trainer from the 2 × 2 study in section 2. No rewards or next states are needed.
  - **Measure against the actual mixture:** compare EqM's samples at each state with fresh samples from the mixture itself (a distribution distance, plus the share of samples near each component). Don't assume two separated groups or a 50/50 geometric split: the two components can overlap and form a single mode. Report how far apart the two policies' actions are at each state, and analyse well-separated states separately.
  - **Optional, controlled separation:** Aware-BC gives nearly identical actions at a state (spread ≈ 0.06), so the components can also be placed by hand, e.g. Gaussians around each policy's action with a chosen spread and distance.
  - **"Good actions" needs separate evidence:** two policies that work on their own trajectories need not both be safe at the same state. Before calling both components good actions, branch the simulator from identical states with each policy's action and compare the outcomes.
- **`halfcheetah-medium-replay-v2`** as a natural example of the two-behavior case, where policies from different stages of training visit similar states. Check its per-state multimodality with the same nearest-neighbour test first.

**Measurement at fixed states:**
- Include held-out dataset states, normally visited states, and states shortly before flips.
- For each fixed state, hold initial noise constant across sampler treatments and record the entire action-refinement trajectory. This separates movement during sampling from changes caused by the environment evolving.
- Estimate local action directions from an **independent data-based reference**, accounting for variation in nearby states. Do not use the EqM Jacobian alone to define the tangent: the field being investigated should not define its own reference.
- Validate the estimated directions across neighbourhood sizes and held-out data. If the conditional distribution has no reliable thin structure or clear covariance eigengap, a tangent/normal decomposition may not be identifiable. In that case, report movement along high- and low-variance directions without claiming they define a true manifold.
- Measure cumulative movement in the estimated tangent and normal directions, field residual, sample spread, proximity to the conditional reference, and saturation. Use the advantage-weighted conditional target as well as the unweighted behavior reference when interpreting changes in distribution fit.

Where the decomposition is reliable, modify the sampler update near the estimated conditional data region:

$$
\Delta a_{\alpha} = P_N\Delta a + \alpha P_T\Delta a,
\qquad \alpha\in\{1,\ 0.5,\ 0\}.
$$

Here $P_T$ selects estimated tangent directions and $P_N$ the remaining directions. Keep the field fixed. Use the same initial noise, clipping, and sampler implementation across treatments; document how a modified direction enters the Nesterov look-ahead.

| Intervention | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Suppress tangential movement | Test the proposed mechanism |
| Uniformly reduce update size | Check whether simply moving less explains the benefit |
| Suppress a random subspace of equal dimension | Check whether the estimated tangent directions matter specifically |

Match the control interventions' update magnitudes as closely as practical. Compare full rollouts using separate screening and confirmation episodes.

**Evidence supporting harmful tangential drift would combine:**
1. Tangential movement occurs during the refinement interval associated with deterioration.
2. Suppressing it reduces flips and improves return beyond the matched controls.
3. The improvement retains a comparable running speed.

**Stronger simulator check:** restore the same **full simulator state**, including relevant time/RNG state, and compare earlier versus later refinement actions, followed by a common continuation policy. This measures consequences without relying on a learned critic's judgment. Use this alongside sustained-intervention rollouts, since a one-action comparison does not capture every effect of repeatedly following a policy.

Focus on movement **before convergence**. Current measurements make substantial ongoing movement after convergence unlikely on the tested dataset states. Evaluation-only interventions still cannot rule out the training sampler having already shaped the critic, weights, and field adversely.

#### 4. Low dimension: separate representation improvements from a dimensional cause

Distinguish two claims:
- A different action representation improves EqM.
- Low action dimension causes the failure.

The first is easier to establish. **Fourier lifting succeeding alone would not prove the second.** A deterministic lift increases ambient dimension while leaving the intrinsic degrees of freedom unchanged; it also changes geometry and the information available after corruption.

**What current results already suggest.** Collapse is just as strong in runs that work (BC weights, medium-replay), and Gaussian AWAC also executes one deterministic action per state, yet scores 93.4. Also, an EqM actor that sampled the advantage-weighted distribution perfectly would give random draws from it, not its average, which is what AWAC executes. So low dimension may be real and still not be the cause of this failure, and a better representation may improve sample quality without closing the gap.

**Step 1: synthetic test (runs alongside the other experiments from the start; a few hours, no RL training).** Use a known conditional action distribution and compute or approximate the optimal EqM regression field, then compare it with the trained network. This distinguishes failure inherent to the objective/sampling dynamics from fitting error. For the cancellation hypothesis, locate low-density stationary points, perturb them to test attraction or escape, and check whether lifting removes the problematic behavior. A small field norm at a midpoint alone does not establish an attracting false equilibrium. Controlled dimension sweeps should specify intrinsic dimension, ambient dimension, and signal-to-noise/separation changes separately. A concrete sweep:
- two-mode mixtures in which mode separation, covariance anisotropy, and mixture imbalance are varied, not one fixed mixture;
- two sweeps: intrinsic = ambient = d for d ∈ {2, 6, 12, 24, 48}; and intrinsic dimension fixed at 6 with a distance-preserving linear embedding into ambient d ∈ {6, 12, 24, 48} (6 dimensions can't be embedded into 2 while keeping distances);
- state whether the noise level is fixed in total or per coordinate, and normalize norm-based metrics across d (e.g. ‖f‖ / √d), so that differences don't come from the coordinate count alone;
- measure ‖f‖ at the data, the sample spread against the true spread, the share of samples on each mode, and stationary points between the modes.

The EqM paper's high-dimensional arguments give no practical cutoff dimension. So a good result at d = 6 on these mixtures would weaken the dimension explanation, but it would not show that HalfCheetah's 6-D actions are high-dimensional enough. Step 2 remains the direct test.

**Step 2: RL comparison** (a low-dimensionality fix, so it needs the project owner's approval, like Experiment 3's #5). Use the same frozen dataset weights from the IQL experiment, and compare:

| Actor representation | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Original 6-D actions | Baseline |
| Larger 6-D network | Control for model capacity |
| Actions plus zero coordinates | Control for extra coordinates |
| Distance-preserving linear embedding | Increase ambient dimension without stretching signal distances |
| Rescaled 6-D actions, decoded before execution | Control for signal-to-noise and scale changes |
| Actions plus Fourier features | Test informative nonlinear lifting |

**Protocol:**
- Start with one added dimension count, such as 24. If the effect is clear, test a smaller and larger count.
- Keep loss scaling explicit and monitor original-action and auxiliary-coordinate errors separately. Match model capacity where practical and report compute.
- Compare both equal sampling compute and adequately converged sampling. A fixed K can unfairly penalize a representation that converges more slowly.
- Measure decoded action quality, distribution coverage, refinement movement, return, and flips. Keep the critic fixed initially so representation changes cannot alter the weights.
- Treat zero padding as an imperfect control: its noisy coordinates can reveal information about the corruption level.
- Improvement specific to Fourier lifting would support an informative-representation effect. Improvement from scaling or extra capacity too would weaken an explanation based specifically on dimension. A successful fixed-weight comparison should be followed by end-to-end training to test whether the benefit survives critic–actor feedback.

**Defer action chunking.** It changes temporal information and the control problem as well as dimension, so success would be harder to interpret as evidence for a dimensional cause.

> **Notes (added 2026-11-04):**
> - This whole section is new; none of these experiments were in the original plan. Their place in the order is in the table in section 1.
> - **Revised after second review:** 5a now uses a 2 × 2 design with matched controls, a fixed horizon after the switch, and narrower readings. 5c is a fresh run (the existing checkpoints can't be resumed exactly), counts flips at the trained sampler setting only, and draws a softer conclusion. 5d is relabelled as a temperature and weight-concentration sensitivity test with low priority: because of the weight cap, sharper weights don't move the Gaussian toward the peak, so it can't separate "average vs peak".

**a) Handoff test** (evaluation only, priority 1)
- **What:** save full simulator states from rollouts of Gaussian AWAC and of Aware (existing medium-expert checkpoints of the same seed; Aware with T = 1 and T = 1/3) at several points in the episode, e.g. steps 100, 300 and 500. From each saved state, continue for the **same fixed horizon** (e.g. 200 steps) with each policy:

| State generated by | Continue with AWAC | Continue with Aware |
|---|---|---|
| AWAC | control | handoff |
| Aware | handoff | control |

- **Design details:**
  - The equal horizon matters: otherwise a switch at step 500 has less time to fail than a switch at step 100.
  - Keep states where a flip has already happened separate from upright states.
  - HalfCheetah's physics are deterministic and AWAC acts with its mean action, so an AWAC continuation from a saved state has a single outcome. Repeat Aware's continuations with several noise seeds per state.
- **Question:** does the flip rate depend more on who generated the state or on who acts after it?
- **Reading (kept narrow):**
  - Aware flips more than AWAC from the same AWAC-generated states: Aware's actions are riskier at those states.
  - AWAC fails after taking over from Aware: this AWAC policy did not recover from those states. It does not show that they can't be recovered from.
  - Aware does better after taking over from AWAC than when in control from the start: this could come from easier states, from skipping the start-up phase, or from the shorter exposure, not necessarily from errors that build up over time.
- **Cost:** about an hour, no training. `drift.py` already loads both policies (`--awac`); it needs options to save and restore simulator states.

**b) Count flips on Minari `medium-v0`** (evaluation only, priority 1; left over from Experiment 3 #3)
- **What:** roll out the saved Gaussian AWAC checkpoints (`/workspace/Trained_Models/checkpoints/AWAC_env_namemedium-v0_*`) and Aware with BC weights (`Aware_env_namemedium-v0_scale_gradient8.0_weightingbc_*`), 3 seeds × 20 episodes, and count flips.
- **Question:** does a normal Gaussian actor also fall over on this dataset, whose behavior policy itself flips in 20.6% of episodes?
- **Cost:** under an hour. `rollout.py` handles the Aware runs; the `Awac_Minari.py` checkpoints need a small loader.

**c) Train Aware for 1M steps on medium-expert** (training, priority 3)
- **What:** a **fresh** run of Aware as in #4 (T = 1, λ = 8, K = 20), 3 seeds, 1M steps (CORL's standard budget) instead of 151k. Keep checkpoints at 151k, 300k, 500k and 1M, and count flips at each with the normal sampler setting only (η = 0.01, K = 20, momentum 0.3). The 198-setting drift grid is not needed.
- **Why a fresh run:** the existing checkpoints hold only the actor and the two online critics (`model`, `critic_1`, `critic_2`), with no optimizer state and no target critics. Continuing from them would restart Adam and reset the target critics, which is not the same as uninterrupted training. The alternative is to implement a complete training-state resume first.
- **Why:** Aware was still improving when it stopped (5,820 at 100k → 7,480 at 150k), while AWAC was within ≈ 3% of its final score by 100k. Part of the gap may be slow learning.
- **Reading:** if Aware gets close to AWAC (≈ 93) and the flips drop, the gap was mostly slow learning. If it levels off well below, the conclusion is only that more training with this configuration did not close the gap. Optimization and hyperparameters remain possible causes, next to a structural limit.
- **Cost:** about 8 hours per run (151k took 69–75 min with 6 runs in parallel). **Needs the project owner's OK:** Experiment 3's rules fix every training run at 151k steps.

**d) Gaussian AWAC: temperature and weight-concentration sensitivity** (training, low priority)
- **What:** CORL's `awac.py` on medium-expert with `awac_lambda` = 1/10 and 1/30 (the tuned value is 1/3; smaller means sharper weights), 3 seeds each, 151k steps. Count flips and saturation at the end. Log the effective batch share, the share of weights at the cap, and the share of near-zero weights (`awac.py` logs no weight statistics yet).
- **What it is not: a clean "average vs peak" test.** The weights are capped, `w = min(exp(A/T), 100)`, so an action reaches the cap whenever A > T·ln 100:

| T (`awac_lambda`) | Advantage that reaches the cap |
|---|---|
| 1/3 | 1.54 |
| 1/10 | 0.46 |
| 1/30 | 0.15 |

- **Why that matters:**
  - As T shrinks, many positive-advantage actions get the same maximum weight. The fit then approaches the average of the positive-advantage actions, not the single best-rated action.
  - Even without the cap, sharper weights change the target distribution itself; they don't just change how the original target's average is extracted.
  - The clean average-vs-peak comparison is the 2 × 2 frozen-weights study in section 2: identical weights, with the Gaussian fitting their average and EqM going to their peak.
- **Reading:** how return, flips, and saturation change as the weights sharpen and concentrate. Compare the effective batch share with Aware's (≈ 3.6%).
- **Cost:** about 45 minutes per run, 6 runs.

**e) Hopper and Walker2d medium-expert** (later, only after a fix works on HalfCheetah)
- **What:** the best Aware variant and Gaussian AWAC on `hopper-medium-expert-v2` and `walker2d-medium-expert-v2`, 3 seeds each.
- **Why:** checks whether the results hold beyond HalfCheetah. In these environments falling ends the episode, so falls show up directly in the score. Hopper has 3-D actions and Walker2d 6-D, which adds another data point on action dimension (a weak one, because the tasks differ).

## Later phases
- **Phase 2 — OOD:** test `‖f(a|s)‖` and the EqM energy as an action-level OOD score, for candidate filtering, reject-and-rank, and conservative critic targets. Compare against GeCO (final field norm), EqC (path-wise energy consistency), and Fisher-metric layer-gradient norms. ⚠ Experiment 2 found ‖f‖ ≈ 3–6 at dataset actions and ≈ 0 only at the sampler's endpoints (the mode), so as trained now, ‖f‖ measures distance from the mode, not from the data.
- **Phase 3 — Offline-to-online:** continue EqM-AWAC online. Consider FINO (wider action support for exploration) and FFM (behavior-tilted fine-tuning).
- **Alternatives if AWAC becomes the bottleneck:**
  - IQL critic: in-sample, never queries actor actions, so there is no EqM sampling inside critic updates.
  - QIPO: iterative support-set weighting.
  - DFQL: Q-gradient injection into the field.
- **Low-dimensional fixes, if drift or cancellation show up:** Fourier lift of the action, Jacobian-gated samplers, or action chunking.
